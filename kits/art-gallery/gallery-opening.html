Starry Swirl

After Vincent van Gogh, The Starry Night (1889) — reimagined at sunset

"A sunset burning through impasto sky — the energy of Van Gogh in a completely different time of day. For the person whose ideas never sit still."

Van Gogh painted movement, not midnight — every swirl follows a current. This kit keeps his urgency and moves it from night to golden hour: sunset amber where the sky was ink-blue, plum where the depth still has to live, rose dust as the color between one moment and the next.

Amber Glow is the anchor; Plum Vortex is the gravity that keeps all that warmth from floating away.

Lily Shimmer

After Claude Monet, Water Lilies series (1896–1926)

"Lily Shimmer. Soft. The beauty is in what dissolves between them."

The anchor isn't the lily — it's the seam, the moment where petal becomes water becomes light, impossible to name precisely. Lily Glow lives there. Underneath, Deep Water holds the pond's actual depth, so the softness has something to rest against.

Quiet, but not passive — Monet painted the same pond two hundred and fifty times. That's relentless, disguised as calm.

Rouge Poster

After Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, Moulin Rouge: La Goulue (1891)

"If the font is big enough, you only have to say it once. The original influencer. Bold lines, flat color, a woman who knows you're looking."

Cabaret Magenta is the specific magenta of a lipstick print on a cocktail napkin — deliberate, not decorative. Charcoal Ink is the outline that doesn't hesitate. Absinthe is the unexpected chartreuse note, named for the drink because it's a little dangerous.

Confidence isn't loud for loud's sake — a great poster is restrained. Fewer colors, more conviction.

Ballet Light

After Edgar Degas, The Ballet Class (1871–1874) and the ballet series

"The grace you see is built on the discipline you don't."

Degas painted rehearsals, not performances — the moments between moments. Tulle Blush is the slipper worn in by a season of class, not fresh from the box. Stage Rouge is the bruise underneath, the evidence of effort the softness doesn't hide.

Mirror Light is the studio mirror finally getting a color — the cool surface every reflection travels on.

Golden Mosaic

After Gustav Klimt, The Kiss (1907–1908)

"Byzantine empress meets modern maker. Pattern on pattern on pattern — and it works. Maximalism with taste."

Hammered Gold is the unifying thread — not shiny-smooth, hammered and handled and ancient. Byzantine Teal is the cool counterpoint that keeps the gold from going costume. Lead Came is the quiet structural metal behind the mosaic, almost colorless, holding every tile in place.

Nine colors here, not eight — density earns the extra slot. This is the maximalist track, named on purpose.

Sunday Dots

After Georges Seurat, A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884–1886)

"Precision with personality — the grid that lets you dance."

Every dot is its own color, never blended — that's Seurat's whole technique, and it's the argument for this kit's five-role grammar. Coral Confetti pops like a celebration against Parasol Navy's structural depth.

Nine colors here too: Afternoon Haze joined as the true cool carrier, the pale sky the brights get to breathe against.

Wave Crest

After Katsushika Hokusai, The Great Wave off Kanagawa (c. 1831)

Momentum, held. Poised force — power that has arrived and is choosing, for one more beat, not to release yet.

The original is Prussian-blue cold. This kit keeps every structural decision — the bold carved line, the flat planes, the foam-claws — and flips only the temperature: sunset coral and amber where the wave was blue-black, deep plum standing in for the indigo's weight, not its hue.

The newest piece in the room, and the line's first non-painterly technique — woodblock, not brushwork.
